# Loneliness Isn't a Male Problem. It's a Participation Problem

Reading note by [Hey Sammy](https://tryheysammy.com/about/) | Published August 18, 2026

Canonical: https://tryheysammy.com/blog/loneliness-is-a-participation-problem/

An editorial response to Slate's *America's Loneliness Trap* on economic access, recurring activities, and why community is more than a social-skills assignment.

![Three people joining a board game around a community table](https://tryheysammy.com/assets/blog/why-adult-friendship-is-hard-hero.jpg)

*A good plan gives people a reason to meet, a way to join, and somewhere to return.*

Loneliness in America is not primarily a male social-skills failure. It is a participation problem: too many people, especially people with less money, have too few affordable and recurring reasons to be around the same people.

That is the important correction in Slate's [*America's Loneliness Trap*](https://slate.com/life/2026/08/men-lonely-social-skills-therapy-economy-crisis.html). Men can be lonely. Women can be lonely. But the strongest pattern is not that one gender forgot how to talk. It is that social connection gets harder when work, money, transportation, housing, and public life leave people with less time and fewer places to belong.

> The question is not only “How do I become better at friendship?” It is also “Where can I go often enough for friendship to have a chance?”

## What does Slate's article get right?

The article pushes back on the popular idea of a singular “male loneliness epidemic.” Recent research from [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2025/01/16/men-women-and-social-connections/) and the [Harvard Graduate School of Education](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/ideas/usable-knowledge/24/10/what-causing-our-epidemic-loneliness-and-how-can-we-fix-it) does not show a simple, dramatic gender gap in self-reported loneliness. That does not mean lonely men are imagining their experience. It means the experience should not be turned into a diagnosis of men as a group.

The deeper pattern is economic. The [KFF survey cited by Slate](https://www.kff.org/mental-health/loneliness-and-social-support-networks-findings-from-the-kff-survey-of-racism-discrimination-and-health/) found that adults with lower household incomes were much more likely to report frequent loneliness. The same article describes how low-paid work, long hours, unpredictable schedules, subcontracting, and gig work can remove the ordinary social contact that used to come from a shared workplace.

That changes the shape of the problem. If you cannot afford the class, the coffee, the train ride, the membership, or the time away from a second shift, “just get out there” is not a complete plan. And if the people around you are always changing, you may never get the repetition that lets a familiar face become a friend.

## Why “work on your social skills” is too small

Social skills matter. Therapy can be useful. Learning how to start a conversation, manage anxiety, or follow up with someone can make participation easier.

But a person can have perfectly adequate social skills and still have no usable social infrastructure. A home health aide who works alone in other people's houses has a different set of opportunities than someone whose job supplies colleagues, happy hours, professional networks, and a predictable schedule. Someone who works six days a week has a different problem than someone with a free Thursday night and money for a new hobby.

This is why loneliness can become a trap. The longer you go without a place to return to, the more effort a new room requires. The more expensive the room, the fewer chances you have to practice. The fewer chances you have, the easier it is to decide that the problem is your personality.

It is not a personality test. It is a design problem, too.

## A repeatable plan beats a perfect social life

The most helpful response is not to demand that people become fascinating, vulnerable, or effortlessly outgoing. It is to make the next small act of participation more realistic.

| Look for | Why it helps | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Low or no cost** | You can return without turning friendship into a monthly expense | Registration fees, equipment, transit, and other hidden costs |
| **A real cadence** | Familiarity needs more than one good afternoon | Weekly or every-other-week dates you can actually keep |
| **A shared task** | The activity gives conversation somewhere to begin | Making, moving, learning, playing, or serving together |
| **A clear solo entry** | You do not need to arrive with a built-in friend | Host, check-in point, beginner rules, and how newcomers join |
| **Room for repetition** | A second visit can be easier than a first | Same place, same time, returning participants, and a next date |

The best activity is not necessarily the most impressive one. It is the one you can afford, reach, understand, and repeat on an ordinary week.

## Where can you start when money is tight?

Start with the social places that do not require a high-income lifestyle. Investigate your local library, parks department, community center, recreation center, volunteer organizations, walking groups, public classes, faith communities, and neighborhood associations. The specific options vary by city, so confirm the current schedule, cost, accessibility, and newcomer expectations with the organizer.

The activity can be modest. A weekly walk is enough. A volunteer shift is enough. A library discussion group is enough. A free practice session at a public court is enough. The point is not to build a glamorous identity. The point is to create a repeatable reason to see some of the same people again.

If a group costs more than you can absorb, that is information about the group, not proof that you are failing at adulthood. Keep looking for a format that leaves enough room in your budget and energy for a second visit.

## Try a four-step participation practice

This week, choose one small plan:

1. Find one recurring activity that fits your actual schedule and budget.
2. Confirm the exact location, arrival instructions, cost, and what to bring.
3. Set a small goal: meet the host, learn one name, or stay through the first activity.
4. Decide before you leave whether the next date is realistic, then put it on your calendar.

Do not grade the first visit by whether you made a best friend. Grade it by whether the room gave you a path into participation and a reason to return. A short conversation counts. Recognizing one person next time counts. Finding out that the format is wrong for you also counts, because now you can change the variable instead of blaming yourself.

## Community is a shared responsibility

The most humane part of Slate's argument is its shift in responsibility. Lonely people still have agency, but they should not carry the entire burden of rebuilding community alone. Family, friends, neighbors, hosts, organizers, employers, libraries, parks, and local institutions all shape whether there is an easy invitation to accept.

That responsibility can be very ordinary. Invite someone to a free walk. Tell a newcomer where to stand. Introduce two people who might get along. Make the next date obvious. Keep the group affordable. Notice who has stopped showing up.

An individual can take the next step, and a community can make that step less humiliating. We need both.

## What Hey Sammy can and cannot do

Hey Sammy cannot make rent cheaper, replace therapy, repair an exploitative job, or create a public gathering place where a city has allowed one to disappear. An app is not a substitute for economic security or civic investment.

It can help with one narrower problem: discovery friction. When you want a fuller social life but do not know where to go, when to arrive, whether solo newcomers are welcome, or what to do next, a concrete plan can be more useful than another abstract reminder to meet people.

The point is not to optimize your social calendar. It is to find one real-world activity that fits your life well enough to repeat. That is where familiar faces start. That is where a city can become less anonymous.
