# The Dinner Party Decline Is a Reason to Show Up Again

By Hey Sammy | Published October 4, 2026

What The Atlantic's new dinner-party reporting says about shared time, and how simple invitations and recurring local activities can help you show up again.

A dinner party gives people a reason to stay in the same room for a while. Someone tells a story. Someone remembers to ask how the interview went. Conversation carries on while the plates are cleared.

Those ordinary gatherings can hold a lot of friendship. When they happen less often, it helps to ask where the next shared hour will come from.

## What the new reporting shows

In his October 1 article, [“Why Americans Stopped Throwing Dinner Parties,”](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/10/americans-socialization-dinner-decline-hosting/688846/) The Atlantic's Derek Thompson examines the retreat from home gatherings. Combining historical and current surveys, he reports that monthly hosting fell from 42 percent in 1975 to 12 percent in 2026.

The underlying [Data for Progress analysis](https://www.dataforprogress.org/blog/2026/9/29/americans-social-activity-has-steeply-declined-in-the-past-30-years) compares 1995 findings with a 2026 survey of 1,098 U.S. adults. For activities reported at least once in the previous year, it found:

- **Gathering at home:** the share who had entertained others fell from 88 percent to 67 percent—a decline of 21 percentage points.
- **Picnics:** participation fell from 58 percent to 39 percent.
- **Club meetings:** participation fell from 45 percent to 26 percent.

The survey methods differ, including sampling, mail versus online responses, and weighting. The researchers describe the comparisons as evidence of trends rather than precise estimates of change. Thompson's longer historical comparison spans surveys too. These numbers point to fewer shared occasions; they do not establish one cause.

## Make the invitation manageable

You can care about your friends and still find the planning exhausting. Hosting means choosing a date, checking everyone's schedule, thinking about food, and deciding whether the apartment has reached an acceptable level of chaos.

It is easy to postpone all of that until a better week. A better week can take a surprisingly long time to arrive.

Start with a plan you can carry out this week. Invite one person for a short walk, suggest coffee after an activity you both attend, or have someone over for the meal you were already going to make. Be clear about the time, place, cost, and how long you have.

A simple invitation could be: **“Want to come over Thursday at seven? I'll make pasta. Let me know what food works for you.”** A plan outside could be just as specific: **“Want to meet at the park entrance Saturday at ten and take a half-hour walk?”**

Adjust the plan together. You are giving someone a usable invitation and enough information to decide.

## Find a gathering you can return to

If you are new to a city, have drifted from old friends, or would like to meet people, an existing local group can provide a starting point. Someone has already arranged the room, the activity, and a way to join.

Look for something you would enjoy doing more than once: a beginner dance social, a board-game group, a community art class, a walking group, or a volunteer shift with other participants. Look at the actual session rather than assuming every group in a category works the same way.

A useful first plan answers four questions:

- **How do I join?** Check whether you need to book, register, become a member, or simply arrive.
- **What happens when I get there?** Confirm the entrance, start time, first-timer instructions, equipment, and access details.
- **Can I come alone?** Ask the organizer whether a newcomer needs a partner or team and which session suits a beginner.
- **Could I return?** Choose a time, location, and price that fit an ordinary week, then check the next date.

A recurring plan gives a good conversation somewhere to continue. You can ask about the project someone started, remember a name, or pick up a topic from last time. Returning creates those opportunities; it does not guarantee that a particular group or person will become a friend.

## Give the first visit a small job

Your first visit can be about learning the room and finding out whether you want a second one.

Try a question tied to the activity: **“Hi, I'm new. Have you been coming to this group for long?”** Ask the host where newcomers should start. During a break, ask another participant what they enjoy about the activity.

Let the exchange be brief if that is how it goes. If you enjoy talking and the interest seems mutual, a small next step can be enough: coffee afterward, arriving together next week, or trying another session.
